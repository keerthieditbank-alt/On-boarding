Section 01
Pre-Edit Planning
1.1 Video Goal — The First Thing You Must Define
Before you open Premiere Pro, you need to understand WHY this video exists. Every editing decision — cuts, B-roll, pacing, music — flows from answering these three questions. Skipping this step leads to random, unfocused edits.
  • Content Direction — What is this video about? What topic, story, or message is being communicated? Read the script or watch a rough cut first.Why: Your B-roll choices, pacing, and text style all depend on the subject matter.
  • Audience Feel — How should the viewer feel while watching? Inspired? Curious? Shocked? Informed?Why: The emotion you want to create determines your music choice, color grade, and cut rhythm.
  • Video Goal — What is the business or content outcome of this video?Why: A "views" video needs a punchy hook; a "sales" video needs trust-building pacing. The goal changes everything.
🎯 Goal Types
Views — viral potential, fast cuts
Engagement — comments, questions, reactions
Sales / Branding — trust, credibility
Impact — emotional, slow-burn storytelling
✅ Before You Start Checklist
Read the script or brief
Know the target platform (YouTube/Instagram)
Know the target video length
Know the audience (age, interest, language)
1.2 Core Editing Objectives — Non-Negotiable Standards
These are the baseline quality standards for every single video we produce. No exceptions. Internalize these — they define our editing style.
1
Maintain Premium Documentary-Style Quality
Every video should look like it took 10x more effort than it did. Clean cuts, intentional B-roll, and professional color grading are mandatory — not optional.
2
Ensure Fast Turnaround
Quality is expected AND speed is expected. Use templates, presets, and hotkeys to work fast without cutting corners on quality.
3
Keep Consistency Across All Editing
Every video from our channel should look like it came from the same editor. Same font style, same color palette, same energy level. Viewers should recognize our visual brand instantly.
4
Avoid Repetitive B-Roll and Animations
Do not reuse the same B-roll clip or animation style within a single video. Repetition kills viewer retention. Every visual should feel fresh.
5
Change Scene Every 10–20 Seconds
The viewer's attention resets every ~15 seconds. Use B-roll, animation, zoom, or text overlays to create a visual change at this interval. This is the single biggest driver of watch time.
6
Video Must Look Engaging + Credible + Cinematic
All three together. Engaging = viewer doesn't click away. Credible = viewer trusts the content. Cinematic = viewer is visually impressed. If any one of these is missing, the video fails.
1.3 Talking Head / Sit & Talk Videos — What the Viewer Should Experience
This video type is the most common format. The speaker is on camera talking directly to the audience. Your job as an editor is to make the speaker look like a professional, authoritative storyteller — without making the edit itself visible.
  • Viewer must never be confused — If a viewer has to pause and re-watch something, you have failed as an editor. Every cut must flow logically.Why: Confused viewers leave. Clear, flowing content keeps them watching.
  • Must have engaging storytelling — The raw footage is just words. Your job is to turn those words into a story with rhythm, tension, and payoff.Why: People don't watch videos — they watch stories.
  • Simple and clean, but premium-feeling — Don't clutter the screen with unnecessary graphics. But every element that IS on screen must look high quality.Why: Over-designed videos feel cheap and amateurish. Restrained design feels expensive.
🚫
Golden Rule: The editor should be completely invisible in the final video. The viewer should only be aware of the speaker and the story — never aware of the editing. If someone watches and thinks "wow, nice editing" — you've broken this rule.
1.4 AI Tool — How to Use It Correctly
We have an AI tool that analyzes the script and raw footage to suggest where to add B-roll, animations, and text highlights. Use it as your starting point — not your final answer.
  • Where to place B-roll — AI will flag moments where a visual reference would help the viewer understand or feel something.
  • Which B-roll to use — Suggestions based on keywords in the script. Always review manually before placing.
  • Where to use animations — Spots where a motion graphic would reinforce the point being made.
  • Where to add text highlights — Key phrases that should be visually emphasized on screen.
⚠️
Important: The AI tool is a starting suggestion, not a final decision. It does not understand the emotional arc of the video the way you do. Always apply your own creative judgment to accept, reject, or modify its suggestions.

Section 03
Footage Review & Story Understanding
3.1 Story Understanding — Answer These Before Editing
Most editors make the mistake of jumping straight into cutting. Before you make a single cut, you need to understand the story. A great editor is 50% storyteller, 50% technician.
  • What is the story of this video?What journey does the viewer go on from the first second to the last? What is the beginning, middle, and end?
  • What is the core emotion?Pick ONE dominant emotion: curiosity, tension, excitement, nostalgia, inspiration, humor. Every editing choice should support this emotion.
  • What should the viewer feel at the end?The ending emotion is the most important. Should they feel motivated? Shocked? Entertained? This shapes how you build the entire video.
📌
Emotion types to consider: Curiosity (mystery, secrets, discoveries) — Tension (conflict, stakes, urgency) — Emotion (personal stories, heartfelt moments) — Informative (facts, data, educational). Most videos blend 2–3 of these.
3.2 Smart Viewing Method — Two-Pass System
Never edit on your first watch. Always watch twice — once to feel, once to analyze. This saves hours of rework later.
Pass 1 — Feel the Content
  • Watch ALL footage at 2x speed without stopping.Goal: Get the big picture. Understand the full story without getting lost in details. Don't mark anything yet.
  • Notice where you feel engaged vs. bored as a viewer.Your gut reaction = viewer's gut reaction. Trust it.
Pass 2 — Mark & Categorize
  • Mark best takes — The delivery that sounds most natural, clear, and energetic.
  • Mark high-energy lines — Moments where the speaker has peak energy, conviction, or emotion. These are your cut points and B-roll triggers.
  • Mark important information moments — Key facts, stats, or points that need visual reinforcement with B-roll or text.
Sort Into Folders
📂 Sort Into These Bins
Cinematic Shots / B-Roll
Establishment Shots
Dialogue / Interview clips
Ambient sound moments

Yellow = Usable take
Red = Reject / unusable

Section 04
On-Location Documentary SOP
4.1 Objectives — What a Documentary Edit Must Achieve
Documentary editing is fundamentally different from talking head editing. The goal is immersion, not information delivery. The viewer should feel like they are physically present at the location.
  • Create immersive, cinematic storytelling — The viewer must forget they are watching a YouTube video. They should feel transported.Use wide establishing shots, ambient sound, natural pacing, and lingering moments to build this sense of place.
  • Make the viewer feel "I am actually there" — This is the highest compliment a documentary editor can receive.Achieved through: proper ambient sound, smooth transitions, matching eyelines, and not over-cutting.
  • Maintain authenticity and credibility — Documentary viewers are very sensitive to fakeness. Nothing should look staged or over-produced.Use natural color grades, avoid unnecessary animations, and let real moments play out fully.
  • Avoid the over-edited YouTube feel — No meme cuts, no zooms every 2 seconds, no constant background music that drowns out the atmosphere.Documentary editing breathes. It gives the viewer space to absorb what they're seeing.
🚫
What NOT to do in documentary editing: Jump cuts every 3 seconds on A-roll, unnecessary zoom effects, over-processed color grades, using the same B-roll twice, cutting away before a moment is complete.
4.2 Core Editing Principle — Let It Breathe
The most common mistake in documentary editing is over-cutting. Real life has pauses, ambient moments, and visual poetry that communicates without words. Your job is to preserve these.
💡
The Breathing Rule: Show moments beautifully → make the atmosphere feel real → tell the story. The video must flow continuously — like a river, not a series of disconnected clips. Let moments breathe. Don't rush to the next cut.
4.3 Story Structure — 5 Acts (Use for Every Documentary)
Every on-location documentary we produce follows this 5-act structure. It gives the viewer a satisfying narrative journey while also keeping them engaged throughout.
1
Hook — Visual + Curiosity
The first 15–30 seconds. Show the most visually striking or intriguing moment from the entire video. Make the viewer ask "what is this? I need to keep watching." Use your best footage here — not your opening footage.
2
Setup — Location Introduction
Establish WHERE we are. Wide establishing shots, ambient sound of the place, introduction of the main subject or speaker. The viewer needs to feel grounded before the exploration begins.
3
Exploration
The main body of the documentary. Moving through the location, meeting people, discovering things. This section can breathe more — slower pacing is acceptable here as the viewer is already invested.
4
Key Insight
The most important moment, revelation, or lesson of the entire video. This is the emotional or intellectual peak. Build toward it with pacing — the cuts leading into this moment should tighten.
5
Ending — Impactful Conclusion
Leave the viewer with something. A final visual, a powerful quote, a lingering shot. The ending is what they remember and what makes them share the video. Don't rush it — give it time to land.
4.4 Shot Management — Purpose-Driven Editing
In a documentary, every single shot must earn its place. There is no "filler." If you can't answer WHY this shot is here, it shouldn't be there.
  • Every shot must have a clear purpose — Does it advance the story? Establish location? Show emotion? Provide information? If not — cut it.Purposeless shots confuse the viewer and slow down the narrative.
  • Visuals must be story-driven, not random — Don't place B-roll just because it "looks nice." It must support what is being said or felt in that moment.Random B-roll breaks the viewer's immersion and makes the edit feel like a slideshow.
  • Maintain speed and consistency — The overall pacing rhythm should not suddenly change without a reason. Abrupt pacing shifts confuse viewers.

Section 05
Editing Flow & Workflow
5.1 Master 7-Step Flow — Follow This for Every Single Video
This is the core workflow. Never skip steps. Never reorder steps. Each step builds on the previous one. Jumping ahead creates rework.
1
Pre-Edit Planning
Define video goal, audience feel, content direction. Answer the 3 story questions. Use the AI tool for initial suggestions. This happens BEFORE opening Premiere.
2
Shot Selection
Watch all footage twice. Mark best takes, high-energy lines, important moments. Sort into labeled bins. Do NOT start cutting yet.
3
Rough Cut
Arrange the best clips in story order. No effects, no B-roll, no music yet. Just get the story working. Focus entirely on narrative flow. If the story doesn't work here, it won't work later.
4
Structure Lock
Review the rough cut. Confirm the story arc, pacing, and overall structure. Make any big structural changes NOW — moving sections, trimming, rearranging. After this step, no more big structural changes.
5
Visual Layering — with Color Labels
Add B-roll, music, animations, text highlights, zoom effects. This is where the video goes from rough to polished. Use color labels in Premiere to track progress by section.
6
Sound Design
Add SFX, ambient sound, transition swooshes. Balance all audio levels — voice, music, SFX. The video should sound as good as it looks.
7
Polish, Recheck & Export
Watch the full video one final time on a fresh eye. Fix any glitch, jump cut, or audio issue. Add any missing element. Then export at the correct settings.
5.2 Rough Cut — The Foundation of the Edit
The rough cut is the most important step. A great rough cut makes everything else easy. A bad rough cut means endless rework. Spend real time here.
Story Arrangement
  • Arrange clips in story order — Follow the 5-act structure or the natural narrative arc of the content. Don't follow the shooting order — follow the story order.
  • No effects at this stage — No B-roll, no music, no animations. Just the raw spoken content in the right order. This forces you to ensure the story works on its own merit.
Music & Pacing
  • Find the right music first — The music sets the emotional tone for everything. Choose a track that matches the intended emotion (curiosity, energy, calm, sadness). Once music is locked, start pacing the edit to it.Music is the skeleton that the visual edit hangs on. Wrong music = wrong feel no matter how good the cuts are.
  • Use beat drops for breathing space — When the music drops or has a break, use that space for slower, more atmospheric shots. This creates natural rhythm in the edit.
B-Roll Strategy
  • B-roll reinforces story AND covers jump cuts — When you cut out a pause or stutter from the A-roll, the jump cut gets covered by B-roll placed on top. Both functions happen simultaneously.This is the core technique that makes talking head videos look professional.
🚫
Strict Rule: B-roll must NEVER repeat within the same video. Once a clip is used, it is done. Using the same B-roll twice instantly makes the video look unprofessional and low-budget.
5.3 Editing the A-Roll — Talking Head Technique
The A-roll cut is the core technical skill of talking head editing. Speed + precision here saves hours on every project.
  • Cut at 2x speed with audio waveform visible — Watch at double speed while watching the audio waveform. Cut on silences (flat waveform). This is 3x faster than normal speed editing and just as accurate.The waveform shows you exactly where pauses and breaths are without needing to listen at normal speed.
  • Start from the strongest moment, not the beginning — The first clip on your timeline should be the most engaging moment from the entire footage. Then build the story from there.Starting strong prevents the "slow beginning" problem that kills watch time in the first 30 seconds.
History Videos (Images Only)
  • Design a graphic layout for each image — Don't just place a raw image on screen. Create a designed frame around it — texture, color treatment, subtle motion, date/context label.Raw historical photos on a plain background look like a school presentation. A designed treatment makes them feel cinematic and credible.
  • Use premade image templates — We have templates in the asset folder for displaying historical photos. Use these and customize per video.
5.4 Hook Rules — The First 30 Seconds Decide Everything
YouTube analytics consistently show that 60–70% of viewers who leave a video, leave within the first 30 seconds. The hook is not just important — it is the entire video. If the hook fails, nothing else matters.
  • Visual change every 4–6 seconds in the hook — The viewer's brain needs constant novelty in the first 30 seconds. A static shot for more than 6 seconds in the hook = immediate click-away.Use: B-roll cuts, zoom effects, animation reveals, text pops — any visual change counts.
  • Energy rhythm like a roller coaster — The first 30 seconds should have a wave pattern: fast → pause → fast → bigger reveal → build. Not flat, not constantly fast.Constant speed becomes white noise. The contrast between fast and slow is what creates tension and excitement.
  • Sync B-roll and animation to music beats — Every visual cut in the hook should land on a beat. This creates a subconscious feeling of satisfaction and energy in the viewer.
  • First 5–10 frames must be on screen immediately — No black frame, no slow fade in, no logo intro. The video starts at full energy from frame 1.

Section 06
Visual Layering & B-Roll
6.1 B-Roll Rules — Quality Over Quantity
B-roll is the visual language of your video. Bad B-roll makes great content look cheap. Good B-roll elevates mediocre content into something watchable. Choose every B-roll clip with intention.
  • Topic-related visuals only — The B-roll must directly relate to what is being said at that exact moment in the A-roll. "Related" doesn't mean "vaguely similar" — it means visually illustrating the specific point.Off-topic B-roll confuses viewers and undermines the credibility of the content.
  • Always prefer real video over animation — If you have the choice between a real video clip and an animation for the same concept, always use the real video. Real footage = credibility and immersion.Animations look like explanations. Real video looks like evidence.
  • For history content: use original photos or dated articles — Show the actual historical photo, newspaper front page, or article with the date visible. This builds instant credibility.A screenshot of a news article with a visible date is 10x more convincing than a stock image of "history".
  • Do NOT use random stock footage — Generic stock footage (random people walking, city skylines that don't match, irrelevant nature shots) makes the video look lazy and disconnected.Viewers can spot stock footage instantly. It breaks immersion and reduces trust in the content.
B-Roll Pattern — Repeat This Every 3 Clips
Clip 1
Normal Cut
Clip 2
Zoom In
Clip 3
B-Roll / Animation
This pattern creates visual variety without requiring constant creative decisions. It's a repeatable system that keeps the video feeling dynamic.
6.2 Punch-In Zoom — Emphasize What Matters
A punch-in zoom is a subtle zoom into the existing frame. It signals to the viewer: "this is important, pay attention here." Use it sparingly for maximum effect.
  • Use on important lines or key statements — When the speaker says something that is the core point of the video, zoom in slightly. This creates emphasis without any text or graphics.The zoom is a visual underlining. Don't underline every sentence — only the most important ones.
⚠️
Over-using punch-in zooms makes the video feel restless and cheap. Use a maximum of 1 zoom every 30–45 seconds. The punch-in preset is available in your Speed System presets.
6.3 Cutaways — When to Use B-Cam
Cutaways are reaction shots or secondary camera angles that cut away from the main A-roll. They add depth and visual variety to interview-style content.
  • Use B-cam footage when available — If a second camera was filming the interview from a different angle, use those clips as cutaways during the edit.Cutaways make the edit feel like a professionally produced documentary rather than a simple screen recording.
6.4 Speaker Eye Line Rule — Maintain Visual Consistency
This is a technical rule about where elements appear on screen. Violating it creates subtle visual discomfort that viewers feel but can't explain — and it makes the edit look amateurish.
  • B-roll must match the speaker's eye line position — If the speaker is positioned on the left third of the frame, the primary subject in your B-roll should also appear in the left third.When you cut back to the speaker after B-roll, the viewer's eye should land in the same place it was before. Eye line mismatches create a visual jolt.
  • Animations without people — maintain consistent positioning — The main element of an animation (title, graphic, icon) should occupy the same screen position as the speaker when transitioning to/from speaker footage.
6.5 Sequence Template & After Effects Usage
Use the pre-built sequence template in Premiere. After Effects should be used sparingly — only for specific elements that can't be done in Premiere.
✅ Use After Effects For
Maps with animated routes
Complex motion graphics
Animated titles / lower thirds
🚫 Do NOT Use AE For
Simple text overlays (use Premiere)
Basic zoom/slide effects (use presets)
Color grading (use Lumetri in Premiere)
🚫
Documentary Rule: The edit should be invisible. Viewers should never notice the editing in a documentary. If an After Effects element calls attention to itself, remove it. Feel over flash.

Section 07
Animations & Effects
7.1 Animation Rules — Less Is More
Animations are powerful when used with restraint. The moment you overuse them, they stop working — the viewer becomes desensitized and the video looks cluttered.
  • Same animation cannot repeat more than 2 times per video — If you use a specific motion style (e.g., the "slide in from left" text animation) more than twice, it becomes a crutch that the viewer notices.Variety signals effort and production value. Repetition signals laziness.
  • All animations must have movement while on screen — A static graphic placed on screen is not an animation — it's just an image. Anything you bring in from After Effects must have ongoing motion (subtle float, shimmer, pulse) while it is visible.Static elements on a moving video feel jarring and unprofessional.
Approved Animation Types
Fade
Smooth opacity fade in/out. Best for subtle text or overlay introductions. Never use a hard cut for graphics.
Smooth Zoom
Gradual scale increase/decrease. Use for punch-in emphasis or cinematic B-roll entrances.
Slide
Directional movement from off-screen. Use for lower thirds, titles, and information graphics.
💡
Template Rule: If you create a new animation that works well in a video, immediately save it as a reusable template. Don't rebuild from scratch every time. Your template library grows your speed over time.
7.2 Text Highlights — Guide the Viewer's Attention
Text highlights are on-screen text that appears synchronized with what the speaker is saying. They emphasize key words or phrases and improve retention and accessibility.
  • Use AI tool to identify highlight moments — The AI tool will flag key phrases from the script that are worth highlighting. Review and approve these suggestions manually.
  • Text animations must be cinematic, not basic — No default Premiere text with a simple fade. Every text element must have a designed appearance — font, weight, color, motion — that fits our visual brand.
  • Every text element must be high quality — If you can't make the text look premium, don't add it at all. Low quality text is worse than no text.

Section 08
Sound Design
8.1 Sound Design Philosophy — Sound Should Be Felt, Not Heard
Great sound design is invisible. The viewer never consciously notices sound effects — they just feel more engaged, more immersed, and more emotionally connected. Bad sound design is immediately obvious and distracting.
💡
The Test: Play your final video without looking at the screen. Does it sound like a professional production? Is there ambient atmosphere? Are transitions smooth? Does the music support the emotion? If yes — your sound design is working.
  • Sound effects must feel natural — not performed — A whoosh effect on a text animation should be subtle, not a Hollywood-style sound. The viewer should feel it subconsciously, not hear it consciously.Loud, prominent SFX call attention to the editing rather than the story.
8.2 & 8.3 Repetition Rules — No Repeating Sounds
The ear is even more sensitive to repetition than the eye. Repeated sounds become annoying extremely fast.
  • B-roll sound effects must NOT repeat — If you used a specific ambient swoosh or environment sound on one B-roll clip, do not use that exact same sound on another clip in the same video.
  • Animation sounds must NOT repeat more than 2 times — If a specific click or whoosh sound is used for an animation, that sound can appear a maximum of 2 times in the entire video. After that, use a different sound for the same type of animation.
8.4 Sound Design Process — Do This After the Visual Edit Is Locked
Sound design is the last creative step before final export. Do not do it during the rough cut phase — you will waste time designing sound for clips that get cut later.
1
Add Ambient Background Sound
For documentary: add the ambient sound of the location (crowd noise, wind, market sounds, nature). For talking head: a subtle room tone to fill the silence between cuts. This makes the audio feel continuous rather than choppy.
2
Add Transition SFX — Swoosh & Click
Add subtle motion sounds to visual transitions (zooms, slides, text reveals). These should be barely audible but create a satisfying tactile feel to the edit.
3
Balance All Audio Levels
Speaker voice should be at -6dB to -3dB. Music should be at -18dB to -12dB (or lower when speaker is talking). SFX should be between music and voice levels. Check with headphones AND laptop speakers.

Section 09
Color Grading & Final Export
9.1 Color Grading Style — Natural + Cinematic
Our color grading philosophy is: "enhance, don't transform." The goal is to make the footage look like the best version of what it actually is — not to make it look like something it isn't.
  • Natural tones — no over-saturation — Skin tones should look real. Colors should be accurate to what was actually filmed. Boosting saturation to 150% looks amateurish and fake.Over-saturated color immediately signals "YouTube edit" to viewers and undermines documentary credibility.
  • Slight cinematic contrast — Lift the blacks slightly (never fully black), and pull highlights down slightly. This creates the "film look" without going to an extreme teal-orange grade.A subtle S-curve in the tone curve is all you need for the cinematic look. Anything more aggressive becomes a style choice that should be intentional, not default.
  • Every element at highest possible quality — Check for any soft focus, overexposed highlights, or crushed shadows in the footage. Use Lumetri's wheels to correct before applying the look.
📌
The color grade preset is already set up in the template project. Apply it and make minor adjustments per clip as needed. Do not rebuild the grade from scratch for every video — that defeats the purpose of having a preset.
9.2 Final Polish Checklist — Before You Export
Watch the entire video one final time with fresh eyes. Ideally, take a break before this final review — your eyes will catch more after stepping away.
1
Full Video Watch
Watch from start to finish without stopping. Note anything that feels off — pacing, visuals, audio. Only stop to fix, then continue.
2
Check for Glitches
Look for: video glitches, jump cuts not covered by B-roll, audio pops or sudden volume changes, text that appears for too short or too long, color inconsistencies between clips.
3
Check for Missing Elements
Is there a spot where B-roll is needed but missing? A text highlight that was mentioned in the plan but not added? A section that feels slow but has no visual change? Fix it.
4
Export at Correct Settings
YouTube: H.264, 1080p or 4K, 16Mbps+, AAC audio 320kbps. File name: SF_<video name>_1st draft.mp4 / SF_<video name>_Final.mp4 (short) or LF_<video name>_1st draft.mp4 / LF_<video name>_Final.mp4 (long). Upload to the shared delivery folder.
